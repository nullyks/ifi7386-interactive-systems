# Activity 03 — Team Formation and Skills Mapping

[← Session 1](../README.md) · [← Previous activity](02-human-centred-design-worked-example.md) · [Academic readings](../../../resources/academic-readings.md)

**Time:** 20 minutes · **Format:** individual reflection → structured team formation → team agreement

## Goal

Form a workable team of 3–5 students while making interests, current contributions, learning goals, availability, and collaboration expectations visible.

## Part 1 — Individual profile

**Time: 6 minutes**

Record brief answers in a note you can show classmates.

1. Which users, settings, or everyday problems interest you?
2. What can you contribute now? Examples include observation, interviewing, visual explanation, writing, organising, presentation, programming, electronics, making, data work, or careful testing.
3. What do you want to learn or practise during the project?
4. When can you realistically work with a team outside class?
5. Which constraints should a team respect? Share only what you are comfortable making relevant to teamwork.

## Part 2 — Structured team formation

**Time: 7 minutes**

1. Speak with several classmates before committing.
2. Look for compatible availability and a shared interest in a user or problem area.
3. Prefer complementary contributions and learning goals. Do not build a team around one technical expert.
4. Form a provisional team of 3–5.
5. Tell the instructor if you are not included or practical constraints make the team unworkable.

Before moving on, check that:

- every student is included;
- the team has at least two possible user or problem areas;
- members have enough schedule overlap to work together;
- contributions are not divided simply into “technical” and “non-technical”;
- no member is made solely responsible for implementation, documentation, or presentation.

## Part 3 — Team record and working agreement

**Time: 7 minutes**

Record in the team's shared notes:

- team members and contact channel;
- shared interests and possible user groups;
- current contributions available in the team;
- skills members want to develop;
- realistic meeting or response expectations;
- one rule for making routine decisions;
- one rule for handling disagreement or uncertainty;
- one rule for keeping work visible and shared.

### Assign today's rotating roles

- **Facilitator and timekeeper:** keeps attention on the question and invites each voice.
- **Recorder:** captures alternatives, decisions, assumptions, and reasons.
- **Prototype maker:** turns discussion into movable and testable representations.
- **Critical friend:** checks logic, scope, safety, inclusion, and evidence status.
- **Presenter:** delivers the pitch and listens during peer feedback.

For a three- or four-person team, combine roles. Roles are temporary and should rotate in later sessions.

## Worked example

A team shares an interest in study environments. One member can organise and conduct careful observations, one is comfortable sketching interactions, one has basic coding experience, and one wants to learn electronics. They can all meet on Tuesday evening. They agree that routine decisions use consent after each member has spoken, concerns are recorded before a decision, and the shared project log is updated after every meeting. The member with coding experience is not assigned permanent ownership of technical work.

## Discuss

- Does every member have a meaningful current contribution?
- Does every member have something they are allowed to learn?
- What will make it safe to question an idea or admit uncertainty?
- How will the team notice if work begins to concentrate around one person?

## Output

A provisional team of 3–5 and a short digital record of team composition, interests, working constraints, rotating roles, and three working agreements.
